Independent review of the supplied spectral reconstruction notebook

The supplied notebook was inspected as reference code. Its widget callbacks and file-writing commands were not executed. The notebook JSON parses as format 4.5, with two cells (one populated code cell and one empty code cell). The saved output is a widget display, not saved numerical TRES results. A full nbformat schema validator is not installed in this review environment. The calculation below was instead evaluated independently from the supplied numeric table.

Meaning of the calculation

For wavelength λ, let the fitted intrinsic decay model be

f(λ,t) = Σk A(λ,k) exp(−t/τk),  t ≥ 0.

Its continuous time integral is Σk A(λ,k)τk. If S(λ) is the steady-state fluorescence spectrum measured under comparable conditions, the notebook forms

TRES(λ,t) = S(λ) f(λ,t) / Σk A(λ,k)τk.

Consequently, the integral of reconstructed TRES over time is S(λ). This is the critical normalization: multiplying the raw pre-exponentials by S(λ) alone would leave a spurious, wavelength-dependent factor Σ Aτ in that integral. The ratio also cancels any positive common amplitude scale applied to a particular wavelength, such as a different count target or acquisition duration, provided that scale affects all fitted components at that wavelength equally.

TRANES is the reconstructed TRES divided by its spectral area separately at each delay:

TRANES(λ,t) = TRES(λ,t) / ∫ TRES(λ,t) dλ.

The provided wavelengths are not equally spaced throughout. The notebook correctly supplies the actual wavelength array to trapezoidal integration. Normalization is over the selected wavelength interval, not the unmeasured full emission band. It is not peak normalization. With wavelength in nm, TRANES has units 1/nm. Peak-normalized steady-state input gives relative TRES intensity per ns; this does not establish absolute radiometric calibration.

Supplied three-component example

The attached data file is comma-separated and includes a header. It contains 13 wavelength rows, 498–653 nm, and the columns wavelength, A1, A2, A3, and steady-state intensity. The matching lifetime order from the notebook is:

  • A1: 114.15 ns.
  • A2: 17.415 ns.
  • A3: 4.613 ns.

Component columns and lifetime values must stay paired; sorting lifetimes alone would produce a different and incorrect result. The steady-state values are already peak-normalized to one in this particular supplied table. They are not a steady-state spectrum for the separate 51-wavelength demonstration measurement.

Independent scalar evaluation at delays 0, 0.025, 1, 10, 50 and 100 ns generated:

  • notebook_reference_tres.csv.
  • notebook_reference_tranes.csv.
  • notebook_reference_summary.json.

Every TRANES trapezoidal spectral area is one to about 2.3e-16 numerical error. Independent adaptive quadrature of the reconstructed decay over time recovers the supplied steady-state intensity at each wavelength, with maximum absolute error 2.23e-16.

Comparison with the new engine gives maximum absolute differences 5.56e-17 for TRES and 6.08e-18 for TRANES. Reversing wavelength order together with the aligned amplitudes and steady-state values reproduces the same sorted result. Independent positive amplitude factors spanning 1e-200 to 1e200 at different wavelengths also leave the reconstructed spectra unchanged, as required by the formula. A known 7 ns tail-reference transformation recovers the excitation-origin reconstruction. These checks are recorded in independent_engine_comparison.json.

Notebook issues to correct in the application

  1. Input parsing: the notebook hardcodes a tab separator and header=None; that does not match the actual comma-separated, header-containing attachment. The application must recognize the real format and require the right number of lifetime values.
  2. Zero areas: the notebook silently assigns zero when a fitted time area is nonpositive, then divides by an unchecked spectral area for TRANES. A positive steady-state intensity with no fitted decay support should instead be an explicit error, as should a zero/invalid TRANES denominator.
  3. Negative delays and invalid lifetimes: the notebook has no adequate bounds or component-count checks. Reconstruction requires finite positive lifetimes and finite nonnegative delays for this causal model.
  4. Cubic interpolation: the notebook’s cubic B-spline display can overshoot even when every input intensity is nonnegative. For the supplied data it creates a minimum near −1.276e-5 at 50 ns and −2.244e-6 at 100 ns. This is an interpolation artifact, not negative emission. Numerical exports should keep the sampled reconstructed values; straight lines or shape-preserving display interpolation are preferable.
  5. Peak precision: at 50 ns the sampled maximum is 603 nm, whereas the notebook’s cubic interpolant reports about 600.12 nm. At 100 ns it reports about 600.64 nm. Such sub-grid peak positions are interpolation-dependent and should not be presented as measured spectral resolution or a precise physical shift without a justified spectral model and uncertainty estimate.
  6. Suggested time horizon: five times the longest lifetime is 570.75 ns in this example. A pure exponential still has exp(−5) ≈ 0.006738 of its initial amplitude there. Whether that is negligible depends on component weight, noise and dynamic range. It is a preview heuristic, not a guaranteed cutoff.
  7. Widget portability: the upload code assumes the newer tuple-style widget value; earlier widget versions use a different structure. A native application input surface avoids copying this version-dependent callback logic.
  8. Export completeness: the notebook exports the TRES matrix only. A clear application export should include TRES, TRANES, sampled steady-state values, lifetime/column pairing, normalization settings and reference-time assumptions.

Conditions needed for physical interpretation

  • A dense uploaded steady-state spectrum may be normalized, then sampled at the fitted wavelengths. Interpolation should remain inside its measured coverage; extrapolation must not silently supply missing spectral information.
  • Steady-state and lifetime measurements should describe comparable sample and excitation conditions. Wavelength response, acquisition duration and spectral bandwidth corrections must be understood. A relative spectrum is not an absolute emission calibration.
  • Fitted nonnegative parallel-exponential models are supported. A reconstructed spectrum does not establish solvation, energy-transfer or sequential kinetics; those may require signed amplitudes or target models.
  • Reconvolution model amplitudes in this workbench refer to underlying excitation-origin exponentials before the discrete bin-average factor. Their intrinsic continuous area is therefore Σ Aτ. That bin-average factor must not be applied again during this reconstruction. External amplitudes with different definitions (e.g. integrated-intensity fractions or bin-integrated coefficients) require the corresponding conversion and cannot be assumed interchangeable.
  • Tail-fit amplitudes refer to the first tail fitting channel. If its known elapsed delay after excitation is d, convert Aref to A0 = Aref exp(d/τ). The absolute displayed detector-time coordinate alone is not enough to determine
    1. Early-time reconstruction from a tail fit is model extrapolation.
  • Lifetime and steady-state interpolation uncertainties are not automatically propagated into reconstructed spectra. Delays beyond the measured post-excitation window depend on extrapolating the fitted model.
